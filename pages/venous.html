Venous Malformations

Examples of slow-flow venous lesions and syndromes, organized by presentation and body site.

Flow: Usually slow-flow Common features: Blue or purple, compressible lesions Model files: Add CAD/3D files to this condition when available

Conditions and presentations

Blue Rubber Bleb Nevus Syndrome (BRBNS)

Definition
Multiple soft, blue, compressible venous malformations, commonly involving skin and gastrointestinal tract.
Causes / pathophysiology
Often associated with somatic TEK/TIE2 pathway variants and abnormal venous development.
Symptoms / key features
Blue skin lesions, gastrointestinal bleeding, iron-deficiency anemia, and fatigue.
Complications
Chronic blood loss and anemia; bowel complications may occur depending on lesion location.
Diagnosis
Clinical evaluation; imaging such as MRI; endoscopy when gastrointestinal involvement is suspected.
Treatment
Individualized care may include iron replacement, endoscopic or surgical management, sclerotherapy, or systemic therapy such as sirolimus under specialist care.
Prognosis
Often chronic and manageable, with course depending on lesion burden and bleeding.
References
Dompmartin A, Vikkula M, Boon LM. “Venous malformation: update on aetiopathogenesis, diagnosis and management.” Phlebology. 2010;25(5):224–235. doi:10.1258/phleb.2009.009041. Efficacy and Safety of Sirolimus for Blue Rubber Bleb Nevus Syndrome: A Prospective Study. 2021.

Facial Venous Malformation

Definition
Soft, bluish venous malformation involving facial tissues.
Causes / pathophysiology
Usually sporadic, arising from abnormal development of venous channels.
Symptoms / key features
Visible or compressible lesion; swelling or pain may occur.
Complications
Thrombosis, localized inflammation, skin breakdown, or functional effects depending on location.
Diagnosis
Clinical examination, Doppler ultrasound, and MRI to assess depth and extent.
Treatment
Observation, image-guided sclerotherapy, or surgery may be considered by a vascular anomalies team.
Prognosis
Often manageable; recurrence or residual disease can occur.
References
Legiehn GM, Heran MKS. “Venous malformations: classification, development, diagnosis, and interventional radiologic management.” Radiol Clin North Am. 2008;46(3):545–597.

Intramuscular Venous Malformation

Definition
A venous malformation located within muscle or other deep soft tissue.
Causes / pathophysiology
Developmental anomaly of venous channels.
Symptoms / key features
Pain with activity, tenderness, swelling, or reduced range of motion.
Complications
Recurrent pain, localized thrombosis, bleeding, or functional limitation.
Diagnosis
MRI is useful for mapping extent; Doppler ultrasound may help assess flow.
Treatment
Compression, sclerotherapy, or surgery may be considered based on symptoms and anatomy.
Prognosis
Symptoms can be managed; recurrence or residual malformation is possible.
References
Dompmartin A, Vikkula M, Boon LM. “Venous malformation: update on aetiopathogenesis, diagnosis and management.” Phlebology. 2010;25(5):224–235.

Gastrointestinal Venous Malformation

Definition
Venous malformation involving the stomach, small bowel, colon, or other gastrointestinal tissue.
Causes / pathophysiology
Abnormally formed venous channels; may occur as part of a syndrome such as BRBNS.
Symptoms / key features
Occult or visible gastrointestinal bleeding, anemia, or abdominal symptoms.
Complications
Blood loss, anemia, and, less commonly, obstruction or other site-specific effects.
Diagnosis
Endoscopy and imaging are selected according to the suspected site and clinical presentation.
Treatment
May include iron replacement and specialist-directed endoscopic, interventional, or surgical treatment.
Prognosis
Depends on lesion number, location, bleeding, and response to treatment.
References
Kassarjian A, Fishman SJ, Fox VL, Burrows PE. “Imaging characteristics of blue rubber bleb nevus syndrome.” AJR Am J Roentgenol. 2003;181(4):1041–1048.; Dompmartin et al., 2010.

Extremity Venous Malformation

Definition
A compressible venous lesion affecting an arm, hand, leg, or foot.
Causes / pathophysiology
May be sporadic or, less commonly, part of an inherited or combined vascular anomaly.
Symptoms / key features
Swelling, pain, heaviness, skin discoloration, or limited movement.
Complications
Localized thrombosis, bleeding, skin changes, or functional limitation.
Diagnosis
Clinical examination with Doppler ultrasound and MRI as needed.
Treatment
Compression, sclerotherapy, or surgery may be selected according to symptoms and anatomy.
Prognosis
Often manageable; some lesions require ongoing care or retreatment.
References
Legiehn GM, Heran MKS. “Venous malformations: classification, development, diagnosis, and interventional radiologic management.” Radiol Clin North Am. 2008;46(3):545–597.
