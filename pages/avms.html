Arteriovenous Malformations (AVMs)

Abnormal tangles of blood vessels connecting arteries and veins

Prevalence: ~1 in 100,000 Type: High-flow Affects: Brain, spine, lungs, liver

Overview

Arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are abnormal tangles of blood vessels that connect arteries and veins, bypassing the normal capillary system. This creates a high-pressure shunt that can weaken vessel walls and lead to hemorrhage. AVMs can occur anywhere but are most concerning in the brain or spinal cord.

Symptoms

  • Headaches (often severe or unlike typical headaches)
  • Seizures
  • Muscle weakness or numbness
  • Vision problems or loss
  • Difficulty speaking or understanding speech
  • Sudden, severe bleeding (hemorrhage)
  • Pulsating sound in the ear (for dural AVMs)

Causes

AVMs are congenital, developing during fetal development. Research points to somatic mutations in the KRAS/BRAF signaling pathway. They are not typically inherited. Some AVMs are associated with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT).

Diagnosis

  • MRI: Initial imaging to detect and characterize the AVM
  • CT Angiography: Detailed view of blood vessel architecture
  • Cerebral Angiography: Gold standard for mapping blood flow
  • Functional MRI: Assess relationship to critical brain areas
  • Spetzler-Martin grading: Surgical risk classification

Treatment

  • Microsurgery: Surgical removal for accessible, lower-grade AVMs
  • Stereotactic radiosurgery: Gamma Knife for small, deep AVMs
  • Endovascular embolization: Catheter-based vessel blocking
  • Combined approach: Embolization followed by surgery or radiosurgery
  • Observation: Monitoring for unruptured, low-risk AVMs

Examples by body site

Presentation, risks, and treatment vary with the AVM’s location and anatomy. These examples are educational summaries, not individual treatment guidance.

Brain AVM (including rupture)

Definition
A high-flow connection between arteries and veins in or around the brain that bypasses the normal capillary bed.
Causes / pathophysiology
Usually a developmental vascular anomaly; an individual cause may not be identified.
Symptoms / key features
May be discovered incidentally; possible symptoms include seizure, headache, or neurologic changes. Rupture can cause sudden severe headache or neurologic deficits.
Complications
Intracranial hemorrhage, stroke-like injury, disability, or death.
Diagnosis
MRI/MRA or CT/CTA; catheter angiography may be used to define vascular anatomy.
Treatment
Observation or a specialist-selected combination of microsurgery, embolization, and/or stereotactic radiosurgery; the balance of risks is individualized.
Prognosis
Depends on lesion features, symptoms, and treatment risks.
References
Spetzler RF, Martin NA. “A proposed grading system for arteriovenous malformations.” J Neurosurg. 1986;65(4):476–483.; Derdeyn CP et al. “Management of Brain Arteriovenous Malformations: A Scientific Statement…” Stroke. 2017;48:e200–e224.

Cutaneous (Skin) AVM

Definition
A fast-flow arteriovenous connection in skin or nearby soft tissue; a lesion may feel warm or pulsatile.
Causes / pathophysiology
May be congenital or, less commonly, acquired after trauma or a procedure.
Symptoms / key features
Warmth, pulsation, pain, enlargement, bleeding, or skin changes.
Complications
Ulceration, bleeding, tissue damage, or high-output cardiac effects in extensive cases.
Diagnosis
Doppler ultrasound and MRI; angiography may be used for planning intervention.
Treatment
Observation or specialist-directed embolization and/or surgery, depending on extent and symptoms.
Prognosis
Recurrence can occur; long-term follow-up may be needed.
References
Amaral JG, Lara-Corrales I. “Vascular anomalies: clinical perspectives.” Pediatr Radiol. 2022;52(2):249–261.. The supplied Richter et al. 2007 paper is specifically about tongue AVMs, not skin lesions.

Limb AVM

Definition
A fast-flow arteriovenous malformation involving an arm or leg.
Causes / pathophysiology
Often developmental; some limb overgrowth syndromes involve fast-flow shunting.
Symptoms / key features
Warmth, swelling, pain, skin changes, or limb-size difference.
Complications
Ulceration, bleeding, tissue damage, functional effects, or cardiac strain in extensive high-flow lesions.
Diagnosis
MRI/MRA and Doppler ultrasound; angiography may help plan treatment.
Treatment
Specialist-directed monitoring, embolization, surgery, or a staged combination.
Prognosis
Depends on extent and response to treatment; continued follow-up may be needed.
References
“Complex management of concurrent lower extremity arteriovenous malformations: A case study and review of interventional techniques.” 2023.; Amaral & Lara-Corrales, 2022.

Incidental / Asymptomatic Cerebral AVM

Definition
A brain AVM found during evaluation for another reason, without symptoms attributed to it.
Causes / pathophysiology
Usually developmental; natural history and risk vary among lesions.
Symptoms / key features
No symptoms at discovery.
Complications
Potential hemorrhage and treatment-related neurologic complications; individual risks differ.
Diagnosis
MRI/MRA or CT/CTA; catheter angiography may be used for detailed characterization.
Treatment
Observation or intervention may be considered after specialist review and shared decision-making.
Prognosis
Cannot be summarized as uniformly “excellent”; depends on anatomy, history, and management choice.
References
Derdeyn CP et al. “Management of Brain Arteriovenous Malformations: A Scientific Statement…” Stroke. 2017;48:e200–e224.

Pulmonary AVM

Definition
An abnormal direct connection between pulmonary arteries and veins that bypasses the lung capillary bed.
Causes / pathophysiology
May be associated with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia; evaluation for associated conditions may be appropriate.
Symptoms / key features
May cause low blood oxygen or shortness of breath, but some are asymptomatic.
Complications
Right-to-left shunting can allow clots or bacteria to bypass lung filtering and cause stroke or brain abscess.
Diagnosis
Contrast echocardiography may be used for screening; chest CT/CT angiography helps define lesions.
Treatment
Transcatheter embolization is commonly used for suitable lesions; follow-up imaging is important.
Prognosis
Often treatable, though residual or recurrent lesions can require monitoring.
References
Gupta P et al. “Pulmonary arteriovenous malformations: effect of embolization on right-to-left shunt, hypoxemia, and exercise tolerance in 66 patients.” AJR Am J Roentgenol. 2002;179(2):347–355.

Spinal AVM

Definition
An abnormal high-flow vascular connection in or around the spinal cord.
Causes / pathophysiology
Usually related to abnormal vascular development; lesion subtypes differ in location and flow pattern.
Symptoms / key features
Progressive weakness, sensory changes, pain, or bladder and bowel dysfunction; sudden symptoms may occur with hemorrhage.
Complications
Spinal cord injury, hemorrhage, or lasting neurologic disability.
Diagnosis
MRI/MRA followed by catheter angiography when needed to define the lesion.
Treatment
Selected by a multidisciplinary team and may include embolization, surgery, or monitoring.
Prognosis
Depends on subtype, location, symptoms, and treatment response.
References
“Spinal and Paraspinal Arteriovenous Lesions.” 2019 review.
Prevalence
~1 in 100,000
Type
High-Flow
Affects
